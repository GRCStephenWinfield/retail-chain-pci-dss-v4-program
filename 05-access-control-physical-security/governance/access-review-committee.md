## 2. The number that matters

The fall from **214 revocations to 9** between cycles is the single most useful figure in this phase. Cycle 1 was a clean-up of years of accumulated access. Cycle 2 measured whether the joiner–mover–leaver process actually works — and nine revocations across 134 assignments is what a working process looks like.

Delgado recorded the counter-argument: a low second-cycle number could equally mean the second review was less rigorous. The Committee accepted the challenge and agreed that **cycle 3 will be sampled by Internal Audit independently** rather than relying on the reviewers' own attestation.

## 3. What "management acknowledgement" required

The Committee spent longer on this than on the results. 7.2.4 requires that access remains appropriate **and that management acknowledges it**. A reviewer clicking "approve" on a list of 1,247 entitlements is not acknowledgement in any meaningful sense.

The standard adopted: a reviewer acknowledges **by role, not by list**, and must state for each role what business function justifies it. Where a reviewer could not state the justification, the entitlement was revoked rather than deferred. **41 of the 214 cycle-1 revocations arose this way** — not because access was known to be wrong, but because nobody could say why it was right.

## 4. The uncomfortable finding

Nineteen of the 63 orphaned accounts belonged to **contractors who had never been registered in the HR system at all**, so no leaver event could ever have fired for them. The JML process was working correctly on a population it did not know existed.

Contractor onboarding now routes through the same identity process as employees. This is recorded as the reason cycle 1's number was as high as it was.
