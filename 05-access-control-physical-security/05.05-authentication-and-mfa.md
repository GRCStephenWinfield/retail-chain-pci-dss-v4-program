## Purpose

This is the largest single workstream in the Marketa PCI DSS v4.0.1 programme. It ran from **2026-02-02 to 2026-06-30**, consumed roughly **2.1 of the programme's 11.4 FTE**, touched **every one of the 71 assessed system components**, changed how **145 identities** authenticate, and raised **five exceptions** — four of which had to be closed before fieldwork and one of which is permanent by design.

It delivers **8.3** (authentication factor management), **8.4** (multi-factor authentication) and **8.5.1** (the properties an MFA system must have). **8.3.9** — password change frequency — is deliberately not here. It is the programme's only customized approach and it has its own document, **05.06**.

The reason this workstream is the largest is a single future-dated requirement that most merchants under-read.

> **8.4.2 is not "MFA for administrators". It is MFA for *all* access into the CDE.**

8.4.1 has required MFA for non-console **administrative** access into the CDE for years. 8.4.3 has required it for **remote** access from outside the network. 8.4.2 is new, was best practice until 31 March 2025, and is now mandatory: **every** access into the cardholder data environment, by anyone, administrative or not, remote or on the corporate network, requires multi-factor authentication. For Marketa, the practical consequence is that **36 Payment Operations business users** — analysts, dispute handlers, settlement staff, a VP — who had never touched a security key in their working lives, and who sit in an office in Columbus on the corporate network, now authenticate with two factors every time they open a dispute case. That population, not the administrators, is what made this a five-month programme.

## 1. Requirement Map

Sections **§2 to §11** take **8.3.1 through 8.3.11** in order — factors, cryptographic protection in transit and storage, identity verification before a factor change, invalid-attempt lockout, initial and reset credentials, the 12-character minimum, password history, communication of policy, the service-provider-only exclusions, and the rule that factors are not shared. **8.3.9 is omitted deliberately and is delivered in 05.06.** Sections **§12 to §14** deliver **8.4.1, 8.4.2, 8.4.3 and 8.5.1**. Section **§15** is the deployment itself, with its exceptions and its operating data.

## 2. Authentication Factors — 8.3.1

| Factor type | Implementation at Marketa | Where used |
|---|---|---|
| **Something you know** | Password or passphrase, subject to §5 to §8 | Universal, as one of the two factors on every in-scope path |
| **Something you have** | **FIDO2 security key** (primary), device-bound push with number matching (secondary), hardware OTP token (three components only), and the sealed dual-custody credential for break-glass | The second factor on every in-scope path |
| **Something you are** | **Not used as a PCI DSS authentication factor.** Biometric unlock is present on some corporate devices as a local convenience gating the platform authenticator; Marketa does **not** count it as an independent factor | Nowhere in the matrix |

The last row is stated because it is a common over-claim. A fingerprint that unlocks a device which then presents a key is not a third factor; it is a local access control on the second factor. Marketa's factor claim is **two types — know and have** — and 8.5.1's "at least two different types" is satisfied on that basis without reliance on biometrics.

## 3. Cryptographic Protection of Factors — 8.3.2

8.3.2 requires strong cryptography to render **all** authentication factors unreadable during **transmission and storage** on **all** system components. Both halves are absolute, and the storage half is where honesty is required.

| Factor and location | Protection | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Passwords in transit, all in-scope paths | TLS 1.2 minimum, TLS 1.3 preferred, with the cipher suites in 04.04 §3. No cleartext authentication protocol is permitted or present on any of the 71 | Met; asserted continuously by **CA-5** |
| Passwords at rest — applications Marketa builds | Salted, memory-hard hashing with per-credential salts; no reversible storage anywhere in bespoke code | Met; verified in code review under 6.2.3 |
| Passwords at rest — the directory (CT-01 to CT-03) | Protected by the platform's credential store. Marketa does not choose the algorithm; it is a property of the product | **Met, with the constraint stated.** LM hash storage disabled, NTLMv1 disabled estate-wide, Credential Guard enforced on all in-scope Windows components, the Protected Users group applied to all 76 privileged assignments, and no reversible-encryption flag set on any account. Verified by **CA-2** |
| Passwords at rest — appliances and vendor-managed components | Vendor-controlled | Attested under 12.8.5 for the vendor-hosted planes; observed as non-reversible where the platform documents it. **This is the weakest evidence in the table and is graded E3, not E1** |
| Vault-held credentials (CT-14) | Encrypted at rest under keys held in the HSM-backed key hierarchy in 04.03; no credential is retrievable in cleartext by a human for the 93 application and system accounts it brokers | Met |
| FIDO2 private keys | Never leave the authenticator. Not exportable, not extractable, not backed up | Met by construction — the strongest row in the table |

The directory row is the one entities gloss over and the one Marketa states plainly: **the algorithm protecting a directory credential at rest is a property of the platform, not a control Marketa configured.** What Marketa can control is everything around it — disabling legacy hash storage and legacy protocols, enforcing credential isolation, and removing standing privileged membership so that a recovered credential grants nothing without a check-out. Those are the controls that were implemented, and they are described as what they are rather than being presented as compliance with 8.3.2 by assertion.

## 4. Identity Verification Before Factor Change — 8.3.3

| Path | Verification required | Volume, 12 months to 2026-06-30 |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-service password reset** | Authentication with the enrolled second factor. A user who still holds their security key resets their own password without human involvement | **19,840** |
| **Help-desk-assisted reset, general population** | Identity proofing against three HR-held attributes the caller could not readily obtain, **plus** a one-time code delivered to the enrolled factor or the HR-held mobile number | **2,440** |
| **Help-desk-assisted reset, in-scope population** | As above, **plus** a call-back to the manager of record on the HR-held number. No exceptions, including for executives | **31** |
| **Factor enrolment or replacement, general** | In-person or video verification against photographic identity, recorded | 3,180 |
| **Factor enrolment or replacement, in-scope population** | In-person verification by an identity administrator **who is not the requester's manager**, recorded, and notified to the role owner | **57** |
| **Any factor change on CT-05 itself** | Dual authorisation under RC-10 — two named administrators | 14 |

Help-desk-assisted resets fell from **3,940 per quarter** before self-service and MFA to **610 per quarter** after. That number is reported here because it is load-bearing evidence in 05.06: **the help desk is a social-engineering surface, and its volume is a function of how often people are forced to change passwords they have not forgotten.** An entity that mandates 90-day rotation is manufacturing calls to the exact function an attacker most wants to talk to.

## 5. Invalid Authentication Attempts — 8.3.4

| Parameter | Requirement | Marketa's setting |
|---|---|---|
| Lockout threshold | Not more than **10** invalid attempts | **10 attempts** |
| Lockout duration | Minimum **30 minutes**, or until identity is confirmed | **30 minutes**, auto-release; earlier release only via the §4 verification path |
| Coverage | All in-scope components | 71 of 71; the 19 locally reconciled realms enforce it at the platform where supported and at the network layer where not |
| Privileged accounts | — | Same 10-attempt threshold; lockout of a privileged account also raises a **P2** alert |
| Break-glass accounts | — | **3 attempts**, no auto-release, immediate P1 |

Marketa sets 10 and 30 rather than something more aggressive, deliberately. A threshold of three attempts across 145 identities converts a low-rate password-spray into a mass denial of service against the people who run the payment estate, and the attacker gets the outage for free. The compensating position is that **the lockout is not the detection control.** Detection is a spray-and-stuffing pattern rule at CT-18 that fires on distributed low-rate attempts long before any single account reaches ten — 05.06 §7 describes it, because it is also one of the controls carrying the customized approach.

Lockouts in the period: **214** across the in-scope population, of which 209 were self-inflicted (a stale cached credential after a password change, overwhelmingly), 3 were a misconfigured integration re-trying with an old secret, and **2 were genuine — a credential-stuffing attempt against two identities whose credentials appeared in a public breach corpus**. Both were detected by the corpus screening in §7 before the lockout fired, and both accounts had their passwords force-reset within 40 minutes.

## 6. Initial and Reset Credentials — 8.3.5

| Control | Implementation |
|---|---|
| Unique value for first use | Generated per user by the identity platform; **no default, no formula, no shared starting password**, and no pattern derived from the person's name, start date or store number |
| Delivery | Out of band to the HR-held mobile number, or in person for in-scope roles; never in the same channel as the identifier |
| Validity | **8 hours**, then expires and must be regenerated |
| **Changed immediately after first use** | Enforced by the platform: the first authenticated action available to the account is the password change; nothing else can be reached until it completes |
| On reset | Identical treatment. A reset credential is a first-use credential |
| Verification | Quarterly control test: request a new account, confirm the initial credential is unique, expires, and forces an immediate change. Four tests in the period, four passes |

## 7. Password Strength — 8.3.6

8.3.6 is one of the 51 future-dated requirements now in force. It obliges a **minimum of 12 characters** containing **both numeric and alphabetic** characters, wherever passwords are used as an authentication factor.

| Population | Minimum length | Composition | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| All enterprise users, ~34,000 plus seasonal | **12** | Numeric and alphabetic | 8.3.6 |
| The **118** in-scope catalogue role holders | **16** | Numeric and alphabetic | Marketa policy, stricter than required |
| The **61** password-only accounts in the 8.3.9 population | **16** | Numeric and alphabetic | Marketa policy; a control in the customized approach — 05.06 §7 |
| Application and system accounts | **32**, machine-generated | Full character set | TRA-8.6.3 — 05.07 §6 |

Beyond the minimum, three additional strength controls operate. None of them is required by 8.3.6, and together they matter more than the length floor.

| Control | Implementation | Effect in the period |
|---|---|---|
| **Compromised-credential screening** | Every password is screened against a known-breached credential corpus **at the moment it is set**, and the corpus is re-run against the stored population **daily**. A match forces a reset | **41 rejections at set** across the enterprise; **7 force-resets** from the daily re-run, including the 2 accounts in §5 |
| **Context screening** | Rejection of passwords containing the user's name, identifier, the company name, store number, or the current or adjacent month and year | 2,118 rejections at set |
| **No composition theatre** | Marketa does **not** require special characters, does not prohibit repeated characters, and does not force a mixed case rule beyond the alphabetic-and-numeric requirement | Deliberate. Composition rules narrow the search space in predictable ways and push users toward the same handful of transformations |

The 12-character rollout across roughly 34,000 people plus seasonal was completed by **2026-05-08** as a natural-expiry migration rather than a forced mass reset, precisely to avoid manufacturing 34,000 help-desk calls in a fortnight.

## 8. Password History — 8.3.7

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| History depth | **Previous four**, per the requirement |
| Marketa's setting | **Previous 12** for the in-scope population; previous four enterprise-wide |
| Similarity checking | New passwords are rejected if they differ from a historical entry only by a trailing digit, a case change or a character substitution — the increment pattern that defeats naive history depth |
| Coverage | Enforced at the directory and at every platform with its own credential store; the 19 locally reconciled realms are verified by the monthly reconciliation, and 3 of them cannot enforce history natively — recorded and carried into 05.06 §7 as a threat to the customized approach objective |

The similarity check is the part that makes history depth mean anything. A history of four defeats a user who cycles four passwords; it does not defeat `Marketa2026a` → `Marketa2026b`, which is what a rotation mandate actually produces and which 05.06 §5 quantifies.

## 9. Communicating Authentication Policy — 8.3.8

| Channel | Population | Frequency | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onboarding acknowledgement | Every joiner including seasonal | At hire | Signed acknowledgement in the HR record |
| Annual policy acknowledgement | ~34,000 plus seasonal | Annual | **97.1%** completion, matching the 12.6.3 figure |
| Guidance at the point of change | Everyone changing a password | Every change | The screening reasons in §7 are shown as guidance, not as a bare rejection |
| Role-specific briefing | The 118 in-scope holders and the 27 external individuals | At role grant and at each six-monthly review | Countersigned at the review |
| Full-PAN briefing | The 14 | At authorisation and annually | Countersigned by Elena Marchetti |

The guidance-at-rejection point is small and effective. A user told only "password rejected" tries a near-neighbour; a user told "this password appears in a known breach corpus" chooses something structurally different. Rejection rates on the second attempt fell from 34% to 11% after the messaging changed.

## 10. Service-Provider-Only Requirements — 8.3.10 and 8.3.10.1

**8.3.10 and 8.3.10.1 are additional requirements for service providers only.** They govern the guidance a service provider must give its customers about password changes, and — under 8.3.10.1, which superseded 8.3.10 from 31 March 2025 — the obligation on a service provider to change customer-user passwords at least every 90 days or dynamically analyse account security posture where a password is the only factor for **customer** user access.

**Marketa is a Level 1 merchant. Neither applies.**

As with 8.2.3, the precision matters for the ROC. **These requirements were never within the 306 applicable sub-requirements**, so they are not among the ROC's **four Not Applicable** entries. Those four are merchant-applicable sub-requirements determined not to apply to Marketa's environment, and Phase 08 names them. A service-provider-only requirement is outside the population, not excluded from within it.

There is a trap adjacent to this and it is worth naming, because it is the single most common confusion in Requirement 8: **8.3.10.1 and 8.3.9 look almost identical and are not the same requirement.** 8.3.9 applies to the entity's **own** user accounts and applies to everyone. 8.3.10.1 applies to a **service provider's customers'** user accounts and applies to service providers. Marketa is subject to 8.3.9 — and meets it through the customized approach in **05.06** — and is not subject to 8.3.10.1 at all.

## 11. Factors Are Not Shared — 8.3.11

| Control | Implementation | Position at 2026-07-15 |
|---|---|---|
| Individual assignment | Every FIDO2 key, OTP token and push registration is bound to exactly one identifier at enrolment and recorded in the factor inventory | **302 authenticators** in inventory — 145 primary keys, 145 backup keys, **12** sealed break-glass authenticators |
| Physical control | Keys are issued in person against photographic identity and are not transferable; a key reported lost is revoked at the directory within 15 minutes and the inventory updated | 9 keys revoked in the period — 6 lost, 3 on termination |
| Logical control | A key registered to one identifier cannot authenticate another. Push registrations are device-bound and a device may hold registrations for only one identity | Asserted at each access review |
| No shared tokens | Zero shared authenticators exist. The only multi-person credential in the estate is the break-glass material in 05.04 §4, which requires **two** individuals and is not a personal factor | Confirmed |
| Backup keys | Held by the individual, not by a team, not in a drawer, not with the manager | Confirmed at Cycle 2 for 145 of 145 |

## 12. The Three MFA Requirements, and the One Everybody Under-Reads

| Requirement | Scope | What it adds | Population at Marketa |
|---|---|---|---|
| **8.4.1** | Non-console **administrative** access into the CDE | The oldest of the three; carried over from v3.2.1 | 15 CDE administrators plus the administrative paths that reach them |
| **8.4.2** | **All** access into the CDE | **The future-dated one.** Removes the administrative qualifier entirely. A dispute analyst opening a case from a desk in Columbus is "access into the CDE" | **All 145 identities**, of whom **36 are non-administrative business users** who were outside 8.4.1 completely |
| **8.4.3** | All **remote network access originating from outside the entity's network** that could access or impact the CDE | Independent of whether the access is administrative | 58 internal remote administrators plus **27** third-party individuals |

The three overlap and none subsumes another. A remote administrator hits all three. An on-premises dispute analyst hits only 8.4.2 — and is precisely the person an entity misses if it reads 8.4.2 as a restatement of 8.4.1.

One carve-out in 8.4.2 is worth recording, because it is frequently cited and rarely applies. The requirement notes that MFA is **not** required for user accounts on point-of-sale terminals that have access to **only one card number at a time** to facilitate a single transaction. Marketa does not rely on it. **The 1,914 P2PE POI terminals are outside the CDE entirely** — the card data path is removed by the P2PE validated solution — so the exclusion never arises. An entity relying on that note should check whether its terminals are in the CDE at all before invoking it.

## 14. MFA System Requirements — 8.5.1

8.5.1 sets four properties. Each is a testable claim rather than a configuration setting, and each is evidenced separately.

| Property | Requirement text, in substance | Marketa's implementation | How it is evidenced |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Not susceptible to replay** | A captured authentication cannot be reused | FIDO2/WebAuthn is origin-bound and challenge–response; the assertion is bound to the relying party and to a server-supplied challenge. Push with number matching is bound to a transaction identifier displayed to the user and expires in 60 seconds. Hardware OTP is time-based with single-use enforcement and a one-window skew | **Ironwood Security Labs** attempted assertion replay and OTP reuse during the September application test as a scoped objective; both failed. Recorded in the test report rather than asserted from vendor documentation |
| **Cannot be bypassed** by any user, including administrative users, **unless specifically documented and authorised by management on an exception basis, for a limited time period** | There is no "skip MFA" path | Conditional access policy admits **no** exclusion group for in-scope resources. Legacy authentication protocols that would bypass the policy are blocked at the identity provider and at the network layer. Fallback to password-only on service failure is **disabled** — the failure mode is denial of access, not degradation of authentication | Policy export asserted daily by **CA-2**; quarterly negative test attempts each known bypass route. **One documented exception exists: MFA-EX-04, the break-glass accounts.** It is authorised in writing by Naomi Bhatt, reviewed six-monthly, and is not time-limited in the ordinary sense — §15.1 explains why, and why that is the correct reading |
| **At least two different types** of authentication factors | Two of know / have / are | Every row in §13 pairs **something you know** with **something you have**. No path uses two instances of the same type — no password plus security question, no OTP plus push | The matrix itself; verified per path in the quarterly control test |
| **Success of all factors is required** before access is granted | No partial success | Authentication is evaluated as a single decision at CT-05. A correct password with a failed second factor grants nothing, reveals nothing about which factor failed, and is logged as a single failed authentication | Negative test per path, quarterly. Four cycles, 80 path-tests, **80 passes** |

The bypass row deserves the most scrutiny and gets it. The failure-mode decision is the substantive one: **if CT-05 is unavailable, in-scope access fails closed.** Marketa considered and rejected a degraded mode allowing password-only authentication during an MFA service outage, on the grounds that an attacker who can cause an outage can then choose the authentication regime. The cost of that decision is that a CT-05 outage is a full stop for Payment Operations, which is why CT-05 availability is treated as a payment-availability dependency and why the eleven break-glass accounts in 05.04 §4 exist at all.

### 15.1 The five exceptions

An MFA programme that reports no exceptions has either a very small estate or an incomplete inventory. Marketa's five are recorded with their closure dates because an exception register that was never populated is not evidence of a clean deployment.

| ID | What could not do MFA | Why | Closure | Date |

| **MFA-EX-01** | Call-recording platform management node CT-60 | The vendor platform supported only RADIUS-based one-time-password authentication and had no push or WebAuthn capability | Hardware OTP tokens deployed to the 3 RC-22 holders; RADIUS path hardened and restricted to the management subnet | **2026-05-12** |
| **MFA-EX-02** | Core switching layer CT-46 and two boundary devices | Appliance firmware offered no native MFA on the local management interface | **Direct administration removed.** The management interfaces are now reachable only from CT-11, which enforces MFA before brokering. | **MFA-EX-04** | The 11 break-glass accounts | By design. An account that depends on the MFA service cannot recover the MFA service | **Not closed. Permanent, documented, and authorised in writing by Naomi Bhatt under 8.5.1's exception provision** — §15.2 | — |
| **MFA-EX-05** | 47 accounts on the CT-19 and CT-22 consoles | An agent version incompatible with the identity provider's conditional access enforcement | Agent upgraded across both consoles; enforcement applied | **2026-04-24** |

**MFA-EX-02 is the one worth studying.** The instinct when an appliance cannot do MFA is to accept the gap and write a compensating control. Marketa instead removed the appliance's ability to be administered any other way, which converts "this device cannot do MFA" into "this device can only be reached by someone who already did MFA". That is the same move as **ADR-0011** in Phase 04 — remove the capability rather than govern it — and closing the exception required proving by test that no residual direct path existed, because a claim of exclusivity that has not been tested is a claim about a configuration rather than about the network.

### 15.2 Why the permanent exception is the correct reading of 8.5.1

8.5.1 permits bypass where it is "specifically documented, and authorized by management on an exception basis, for a limited time period". Marketa's break-glass exception is documented and authorised, and is **not** limited to a fixed calendar period. The programme's reading, put to Grant Whitfield at the April checkpoint and accepted:

| Reading | Position |
|---|---|
| "Limited time period" means the exception must expire on a date | Rejected. Under that reading, a recovery control must be periodically removed from a production estate and re-authorised, which introduces a scheduled window with no recovery path |
| "Limited time period" bounds **the use**, not the authorisation | **Adopted.** The credential does not exist in a usable state; it is released for a specific event, is valid for at most 4 hours, rotates on check-in, and every release is a P1 event with two named humans attached. The bypass is time-limited to the incident |
| The authorisation itself is unbounded | **No.** It is reviewed at every six-monthly access review and re-signed annually by the CISO. An authorisation reviewed twice a year is not a standing grant |

This is recorded as **ADR-0015** so that a future assessor sees the reasoning rather than a policy that appears to have forgotten to set an expiry date.

### 15.3 What the operating period actually showed

| Observation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Two suspected MFA-fatigue attempts**, June 2026 | Two identities received clusters of unsolicited push prompts. **Number matching denied both** — the user cannot approve without reading a number from the legitimate screen. Both raised as security incidents, both traced to credential-stuffing using breach-corpus passwords, both accounts force-reset. This is the concrete argument for number matching over simple approve/deny push, and it is why simple push is not used anywhere in the matrix |
| **31 step-up denials** | Authentications denied by risk signals — impossible travel, an unrecognised device on a privileged path, an anomalous authentication hour on a service-adjacent identity. 29 were legitimate users in unusual circumstances and were resolved through the §4 verification path; 2 were the credential-stuffing attempts above |
| **Zero successful authentications with one factor** on any in-scope path | The claim an assessor will test hardest. Evidenced by CT-05 authentication records over the full period rather than by policy configuration |
| The friction cost | 41 complaint tickets in the six weeks after 8.4.2 enforcement, falling to 2 in the following six weeks once security keys replaced push for the highest-frequency population — 05.04 §5 |

### 15.4 What made this hard, and what it treats

The technical work was not the difficulty. Deploying an MFA service, issuing keys and writing conditional access policy is a known problem with known tools. Three things made this a five-month programme.

**The population that had never been in scope before.** 8.4.1 has always covered administrators, and administrators expect MFA. 8.4.2 brought in 36 business users whose entire working day is inside the tool that now challenges them — and whose re-authentication frequency, under 8.2.8's 15-minute idle rule, is more than eleven times a day. The programme's largest single win was not a control; it was replacing push with a security key tap for that population and taking the median re-authentication from 22 seconds to 4.

**The components somebody else builds.** MFA-EX-01, -02 and -03 are all the same shape: an appliance or a vendor platform that cannot present a second factor. Three different closure strategies were needed and none of them was a setting.

**Proving the negative.** "MFA cannot be bypassed" is not evidenced by a policy screenshot. It is evidenced by attempting every known bypass — legacy protocol fallback, service-failure degradation, an exclusion group, direct appliance administration, assertion replay — and failing. That testing, and the Ironwood replay work in September, is most of what makes 8.5.1 defensible.

| Risk treated | How | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| **R-18** — privileged access to an NSC management plane obtained from a workstation that also carries general network and email access | MFA-13 forces every G10 administrative path through CT-11 with a security key; MFA-EX-02 removed the direct path entirely | **Held at Moderate 12.** The control is two months old. Movement is a Phase 06 question, once the session records have accumulated |
| **R-21** — phishing delivers a foothold used to reach administrative services | Phishing-resistant FIDO2 on every administrative path; number matching everywhere push is used; two fatigue attempts denied in the period | **Held at Moderate 12.** Two denied attempts is encouraging and is not an operating history |
| **R-27** — compromise of the administrative-services zone yields every path into the CDE at once | MFA on all 20 paths, zero standing privileged rights, session recording | **Held at High 15.** MFA raises the cost of the first step; it does not change the concentration that makes the zone worth attacking |

Every one is **held**, not reduced. This is the same discipline Phase 04 applied: **a control deployed three months ago is a design, not a demonstrated reduction.** The September penetration test and Phase 06's monitoring evidence are what will move them.

## 16. What This Document Does Not Claim

| Claim | Position |
|---|---|
| That MFA prevents CDE compromise | It raises the cost of credential-based entry on 20 paths. It does nothing against a compromised session, a malicious insider who authenticates correctly, or a flaw in an application the authenticated user is entitled to reach |
| That 8.4.2 is fully proved by 100% enrolment | Enrolment is coverage. Effectiveness is evidenced by the negative testing in §14, the replay testing in September, and the absence of any single-factor authentication in the CT-05 record — and all three are four months old at most |
| That the factor storage claim is uniformly strong | §3 grades the directory row and the appliance row honestly. FIDO2 private keys are the strongest evidence in the estate and vendor attestation is the weakest, and averaging them into a single statement would hide that |
| That five exceptions is the complete population | It is the population found by walking all 20 paths across 71 components. An access path nobody enumerated would not appear here, which is why the coverage reconciliation in §13.1 is component-by-component rather than a percentage |
| That the permanent break-glass exception is beyond challenge | §15.2 sets out the reasoning and records it as ADR-0015 precisely so it can be challenged. A QSA reading "limited time period" strictly would require a different arrangement, and the evidence supports that conversation |
