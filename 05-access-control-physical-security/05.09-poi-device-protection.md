## Purpose

**1,914 point-of-interaction terminals. 482 stores. 38 states. 74% of Marketa's transaction volume, roughly 50.6 million card presentations a year, begins on one of these devices.** They stand on public counters, are handled by strangers all day, and are alone in the dark in 482 unlocked-by-morning buildings every night.

This document delivers **Requirement 9.5.1** and its three sub-requirements:

| Sub-requirement | The obligation, stated plainly |
|---|---|
| **9.5.1** | POI devices that capture payment card data through **direct physical interaction** with the payment card form factor are **protected from tampering and from substitution** |
| **9.5.1.1** | An **up-to-date list of POI devices** is maintained, including make, model, location, and **serial number or other method of unique identification** |
| **9.5.1.2** | **Device surfaces are periodically inspected** to detect tampering and to detect substitution |
| **9.5.1.2.1** | **The frequency and type of those inspections is defined in the entity's targeted risk analysis performed according to 12.3.1** |
| **9.5.1.3** | **Personnel in POI environments are trained** to be aware of attempted tampering or replacement of devices — including verifying the identity of third-party repair personnel, not installing or replacing devices without verification, being aware of suspicious behaviour, and reporting it |

It also delivers **TRA-9.5.1.2.1** in full — the fifth of the programme's fourteen targeted risk analyses under 12.3.1 — in §5.

Two things about this requirement are unlike anything else in the programme, and they shape every design decision below.

> **First: P2PE does not help here.** The Verition P2PE solution removes 1,914 lanes and 482 servers from the cardholder data environment because account data is enciphered inside the device before it goes anywhere. **9.5.1 is the requirement that guards the assumption P2PE rests on** — that the device doing the enciphering is the device Marketa installed, and that nothing has been added to it. A substituted or overlaid device captures data **before** the SRED function ever sees it. Scope reduction and 9.5.1 are not alternatives; the second is the price of the first.
>
> **Second: this control is executed by approximately 29,600 store colleagues, not by security professionals.** It is not performed by visiting engineers, it is not automated, it cannot be asserted from a console, and for the colleague performing it, it is somewhere around the twentieth thing on their shift. Its quality is a function of **training and simplicity**, not of policy. §9 is about that, and it is the honest part of this document.

**R-02** — POI device tampering, skimmer overlay or whole-device substitution across 1,914 terminals in 482 buildings that are unattended overnight and staffed by a workforce that turns over seasonally — is scored **High 15** and is treated here.

## 1. What Is Actually Being Defended Against

A skimming attack on a card-present estate is not one threat. It is four, with different economics, different indicators and different detection controls, and conflating them produces an inspection regime that looks for the wrong things.

| Threat | How it works | What detects it | Does the quarterly inspection catch it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Internal tamper** — the device is opened and a tap is placed on the reader or the keypad matrix | Requires opening the case | **The device itself.** A SRED-capable POI has tamper-response circuitry that zeroises key material on case intrusion and stops transacting. Verition's estate telemetry reports the tamper state of every device to CT-49 within minutes | **No, and it does not need to.** The device is dead and the store has called the help desk before anyone inspects anything |
| **Overlay skimmer** — a shell fitted over the card slot or a membrane over the keypad, capturing the magnetic stripe or the PIN, storing or transmitting it separately | Nothing is opened. The device transacts normally, reports normally, and telemetry sees nothing at all | **Human eyes and human hands.** Nothing else in Marketa's estate can see it | **Yes. This is the threat the inspection exists for** |
| **Whole-device substitution** — the terminal is swapped for a visually similar unit that captures data and forwards a legitimate-looking transaction, or simply captures and declines | Requires unsupervised physical access for a minute or two | **The serial number.** A substituted device looks right, feels right and may even transact; it cannot have the serial the register expects | **Yes — via the serial check, not the visual check** |
| **Added peripheral** — an inline device on the cable, a second reader, a hidden camera covering the PIN pad | Requires access behind or above the counter | Cable inspection; visual sweep of the lane's surroundings; POI VLAN flow analysis where the addition is networked | **Yes for the physical ones; flow analysis for the networked ones** |

Two consequences follow, and the whole regime is built on them.

**The inspection is not the primary detection control for most POI attacks.** Tamper response and telemetry are faster, cheaper and more reliable for anything that involves opening the device. The inspection uniquely catches the **overlay** and the **substitution** — the two attacks that leave no electronic signature whatsoever. Designing the inspection to look for everything makes it worse at the two things only it can find.

**The serial check is not a bureaucratic step. It is the entire substitution control.** An inspector looking at a substituted terminal sees a terminal. The only thing that distinguishes it from the right one is a number, and the only way that number means anything is if there is an accurate list to compare it against — which is why 9.5.1.1 comes before 9.5.1.2 in the standard and in this document.

## 2. The Eight Controls

| ID | Control | Requirement anchor |
|---|---|---|
| **POI-1** | **The register is the source of truth, and it is reconciled to physical reality rather than to itself.** Make, model, location, serial and firmware for all 1,914 devices, reconciled monthly against Verition telemetry and quarterly against a human reading a label | 9.5.1.1 |
| **POI-2** | **Every device movement is a chain-of-custody event** — receipt, deployment, relocation, service, return, destruction. A device with a gap in its custody history is treated as a device of unknown provenance | 9.5.1, PIM-03, PIM-04 |
| **POI-3** | **Quarterly full-population inspection**, five checks, visual and serial, at every one of the 1,914 terminals, performed by a store colleague on shift and recorded per terminal with a photograph | 9.5.1.2, 9.5.1.2.1 |
| **POI-4** | **Event-driven inspection** on receipt, on return from service, on any suspicious-behaviour report, after any unescorted contractor visit to a trading area, and estate-wide on any brand or Verition skimming advisory | 9.5.1.2 |
| **POI-5** | **No device is installed, replaced, opened or removed without a verified work order**, and the colleague verifies the engineer against the work order before the engineer touches anything | 9.5.1.3 |
| **POI-6** | **Training for every colleague in a POI environment** within 14 days of hire, before annual refresh, with a quarterly three-question in-app check | 9.5.1.3 |
| **POI-7** | **A no-consequence reporting channel** for anything that looks odd, available on the device every colleague already carries, triaged within one hour during trading | 9.5.1.3, 12.10.4 |
| **POI-8** | **Suspected tamper is an incident, not a maintenance ticket.** Escalation to Marcus Hale and Adaeze Nwosu, notification to Verition within the PIM's timescale, and the terminal removed from service pending disposition | 9.5.1, PIM-08, 12.10.1 |

POI-8 is the one that separates a working regime from a paper one. The natural handling of a broken seal in a retail estate is to raise a maintenance ticket, because in the overwhelming majority of cases that is what it turns out to be. **The default disposition must nonetheless be "suspected tamper", and the terminal must come out of service before anybody knows the answer**, because the alternative is a process in which the one real event is triaged by the same reflex that correctly handled the ninety-nine false ones. §8 is what happened when this was tested.

## 3. The Device List — 9.5.1.1

### 3.1 What is held

| Attribute | Held? | Source of truth | Refresh |
|---|---|---|---|
| Make and model | Yes | Verition estate management, reconciled to physical | Monthly |
| **Serial number** | Yes — **both** the printed label serial **and** the device-reported serial from telemetry | Physical label at inspection; Verition telemetry | Quarterly / daily |
| Location — store number, lane identifier, position | Yes | Store Operations device register | On movement |
| Firmware version | Yes | Verition telemetry | Daily |
| Key injection reference and P2PE solution component identity | Yes | Verition provisioning record | On deployment |
| Deployment date and installation wave | Yes | Device movement log | On movement |
| **Last inspection date, inspector identity and outcome** | Yes | The inspection record — §6 | Quarterly |
| Chain-of-custody history | Yes | Device movement log | On movement |
| Tamper-evident seal identifier | Yes | Applied at deployment or reseal; recorded per device | On application |

Holding **two** serials rather than one is the detail that makes the register defensible. The label serial is what a colleague can read; the device-reported serial is what the device asserts about itself over telemetry. **A substitution that fools the label check fails the telemetry reconciliation, and a telemetry spoof fails the label check.** They are reconciled monthly, and any device where the two disagree is an immediate POI-8 escalation. Disagreements in the twelve months to 2026-06-30: **zero**, after the seven historical cases in §4 were closed.

They are inventoried monthly against the same register, stored in a locked cage inside the DC, and drawn down against a work order.

## 4. The Seven Serial Mismatches Carried From Phase 02

02.04 §7 reported the February 2026 reconciliation across all 1,914 devices: **1,907 matched; seven had a serial number mismatch between the register and the physical device.** All seven were traced to like-for-like swaps performed by field engineering during fault replacement where the new serial was never recorded. The register was corrected and the swap procedure was changed to require serial capture before a replacement is put into service.

Phase 05 inherited them, and inheriting a corrected finding is not the same as closing it. Correcting a register entry proves the register now says what the device says. It does not prove the device is the device Verition supplied. Those are different claims, and only the second one matters for 9.5.1.

| Verification performed on each of the seven | Result |
|---|---|
| Physical re-verification at the Q1 inspection — label serial photographed, device-reported serial pulled from telemetry, both compared to the corrected register | **7 of 7 match** |
| Model and firmware confirmed inside the listed P2PE solution component set | **7 of 7** |
| Verition asked to confirm each serial as a device **supplied and key-injected under the listed solution** | **7 of 7 confirmed** from Verition's provisioning records |
| Chain of custody reconstructed from the RMA record naming the replaced serial | **5 of 7** |
| Chain of custody reconstructed from **other sources** — the field engineer's job record and the store's own maintenance log | **2 of 7** |
| Full-population reconciliation repeated at Q2 and Q3 | **1,914 of 1,914 match** at both |

The two devices without an RMA record are the honest residual and they were escalated as such rather than filed with the other five. Both were confirmed by Verition as legitimately supplied and key-injected, which is the substantive assurance. Both were confirmed in place, sealed and transacting normally. What is missing is not the device's provenance but **Marketa's own contemporaneous record of it**, reconstructed after the fact from two systems that were never designed to be a custody chain.

| Action | Owner | Date | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seven devices individually re-verified against physical, telemetry and Verition provisioning records | Adaeze Nwosu | 2026-04-30 | Closed |

Seven mismatches in 1,914 is a 0.37% register error rate, none of them a substitution. The programme reports it anyway and gives it a section, for the reason 02.04 gave and this document restates in stronger terms: **a like-for-like swap and a substitution are the same technical event performed with different intent.** An inventory process that cannot notice the first is structurally incapable of noticing the second. The 0.37% is not the finding. The finding is that for a period of months the estate's substitution detection had a measurable false-negative rate and nobody knew the number.

### 5.1 Element 1 — Assets being protected

The **1,914 deployed POI terminals** and the **74 spare devices**; the account data of approximately **50.6 million card-present transactions a year** presented to them; the **P2PE scope reduction** that removes 482 store back-office servers and 496 store-estate network appliances from the cardholder data environment and which condition **P2PE-C7** makes conditional on this very regime; and Marketa's standing with **Cardinal Merchant Bank** and the card brands, for whom a skimming event at a national retailer is a portfolio-level event rather than a store-level one.

### 5.2 Element 2 — Threats the requirement protects against

The four threats enumerated in §1, with two additions the periodic inspection does not itself address.

| Threat | Realisation, and what is known about it |
|---|---|
| The four in **§1** | Overlay, substitution, added peripheral and internal tamper, with the detection asymmetry §1 sets out — the first three need a human, the fourth defeats itself |
| **Compromise via a person presenting as a service engineer** | The social route. Cheaper than the physical route and historically the more successful one across the retail sector. Treated by **POI-5** and tested in §7.3 |
| **Tampering during the supply chain or during service** | A device compromised before it reaches the store. Treated by chain of custody, by Verition's key injection and by inspection on receipt |

### 5.3 Element 3 — Factors contributing to likelihood and impact

| Factor | Direction | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **1,914 devices on public counters in 482 buildings across 38 states** | **Strongly increases** likelihood | Physical exposure is not reducible. Every terminal is, by design, within arm's reach of a member of the public for the whole trading day |
| **Buildings are unattended overnight** | Increases | 482 sites, most in multi-tenant retail locations with cleaning, maintenance and landlord access outside trading hours |
| **The workforce turns over, and grows by 6,800 between October and January** | **Strongly increases** | **CON-09.** The quarter with the highest transaction volume is also the quarter when the largest share of the people watching the devices joined most recently. **R-13** |
| Devices are SRED-capable with active tamper response | **Strongly reduces** impact of the internal-tamper class | Key material zeroises on intrusion; the device stops transacting. This removes the entire "opened device" threat from the inspection's job |
| **P2PE means a successful overlay yields track or keypad data, not the store's transaction stream** | Reduces impact — but not to zero | An overlay captures the card at the point of physical interaction, upstream of encryption. **The scope reduction does not protect the customer** |
| Verition telemetry reports tamper state, firmware and device-reported serial daily | Reduces likelihood of undetected internal compromise | Continuous; independent of the inspection |
| **Nothing except a human sees an overlay** | **Strongly increases** residual likelihood | The decisive factor in the determination. Between inspections, the only overlay detection Marketa has is a colleague or a customer noticing |
| Inspection is performed by non-specialists on shift, with turnover | Increases likelihood of a **missed** indicator | Mitigated by simplicity of design, not by additional steps — §9 |
| **CON-02 — every store-side activity is a 482× operation** | Constrains frequency | An inspection design requiring an engineer visit is not deliverable at any frequency |
| Seven register mismatches found in February 2026 | Increases | Direct evidence that the substitution-detection layer had a measurable error rate — §4 |
| Six seal discrepancies found in Q3 2026, none tampering | Neutral to slightly reducing | Evidence the regime raises things. **Not** evidence it would raise the right thing |

### 5.4 Element 4 — The determination and its justification

**Type: full visual and serial verification at 100% of terminals. Frequency: quarterly, plus the POI-4 event triggers.**

| Parameter | Determination | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| **Coverage** | **100% of the 1,914 terminals every cycle. Not a sample** | Sampling is a technique for estimating a rate in a population. **The population Marketa cares about has an expected size of one.** A 10% sample of 1,914 terminals has a 90% chance of missing a single overlay, and the number that matters is not the rate |
| **Type — visual** | The **five-point check** at §6.2: serial, seal, surface, cable, fit | Five checks a colleague can perform in ninety seconds and remember without the card in front of them. §9 explains why five and not fifteen |
| **Type — serial** | **Label serial read by the inspector and compared to the register in the app**, plus monthly telemetry reconciliation of the device-reported serial | The only control that detects substitution. A visual-only inspection meets 9.5.1.2's words and does not meet its purpose |
| **Type — evidence** | A **photograph** of the device and its seal, captured in the app, geo-bound to the store and stamped with the colleague's identity and the time | An inspection producing no dated, attributable artefact did not happen — 04.13 §6.3 |
| **Frequency — quarterly** | Four cycles a year, aligned to Marketa's trading quarters | Below |
| **Frequency — event-driven** | **POI-4** in full | The half of the determination that responds to information rather than to the calendar |

**Why not more often than quarterly.** Monthly inspection of 1,914 terminals is 22,968 inspections a year, roughly 610 hours of colleague time, performed in stores that are also running a trading day. The marginal detection benefit is real but small: the mean exposure window for an overlay falls from about 45 days to about 15. The cost is not the hours. **The cost is that a task performed monthly by a busy non-specialist becomes routine noise**, and the quality proxies in §9.3 — dwell time and photograph quality — are the mechanism by which the programme can see that happening. Marketa's judgement, recorded and reviewable, is that four attentive inspections beat twelve perfunctory ones, and the analysis states the assumption plainly so that the 2027 review can test it against a year of dwell-time data.

**Why not annually or semi-annually.** An overlay operating for up to twelve months against a store's full card-present volume is not a bounded exposure. The P2PE Instruction Manual anticipates a materially shorter cycle, and condition **P2PE-C7** makes the inspection regime part of the basis for the estate's scope reduction. A cadence that made the exposure window annual would call the reduction into question on its own.

**Why the event triggers carry as much weight as the cadence.** The quarterly cycle is a clock. The POI-4 triggers respond to information: a device coming back from service, a colleague's report, a contractor who was alone in a trading area, a brand advisory about skimming activity in a region. In the period, **event-driven inspections outnumbered nothing and detected nothing** — 214 of them, 0 findings — and the programme keeps them anyway, because the argument for the trigger is not its hit rate but the fact that the alternative is waiting up to 90 days after learning something.

### 5.5 Element 5 — Annual review of this analysis

Reviewed at least once every 12 months, next due **2027-04-21**, by Adaeze Nwosu with Trevor Kim and Marcus Hale, approved by Naomi Bhatt. The review tests four things: whether the dwell-time and photograph-quality data support or undermine the four-attentive-inspections judgement; whether the estate's device population, model set or store count has changed materially; whether any brand, acquirer or Verition guidance has moved; and whether the event-trigger set has produced anything, because a trigger that has never fired in two years is either well designed or wrongly specified and the analysis has to say which.

### 5.6 Element 6 — Triggers for an updated analysis before the annual date

| Trigger | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Any confirmed tampering or substitution anywhere in the estate** | Re-analyse in full and immediately. The determination's central assumption — that no event has occurred — would be false |
| A Verition, brand or acquirer advisory of skimming activity affecting the deployed device models or Marketa's regions | Re-analyse the frequency for the affected population; estate-wide event-driven inspection under POI-4 in the meantime |
| A change of device model, of the P2PE solution, or of the solution's listing status | Re-analyse. The tamper-response and telemetry assumptions in §5.3 are properties of the device |
| **Store count changes by more than 10%, or the estate exceeds 2,200 terminals** | Re-analyse the deliverability of 100% coverage |
| Two consecutive cycles below **97% coverage** | Re-analyse. A cadence that is not being achieved is not the cadence |
| The dwell-time or photograph-rejection measures degrade by more than a third across two cycles | Re-analyse the type. Quality has moved, and frequency and type trade against each other |
| A material change to the seasonal workforce model | Re-analyse. **R-13** and **CON-09** are inputs to this determination, not background |

## 6. What a Colleague Actually Does

### 6.1 The design constraints, stated before the design

| Constraint | Consequence |
|---|---|
| The inspector is a store colleague on shift, not a security professional | No jargon, no judgement calls requiring expertise, no reference to a threat model |
| Median store tenure is short and 6,800 people join in a single quarter | The task must be learnable in one sitting and re-learnable from the aid at the lane |
| **CON-02** — 482 stores | Nothing that needs an engineer, a laptop, a tool or a shipment |
| The task competes with customers | Ninety seconds a terminal, six and a half minutes a store, done between customers rather than instead of them |
| The output must be evidence a QSA can sample in November | Dated, attributable, per-terminal, with an artefact |
| A colleague who is unsure must have somewhere to go that costs them nothing | POI-7, and it must be genuinely consequence-free |

### 6.2 The five checks

The inspection is on the handheld device the colleague already carries — the same 802.1X-authenticated store handheld covered in 03.04 — as a task in the workforce app, alongside the other things a shift asks for. There is no separate login, no separate device and no paper.

| ID | Check | What the app says, in the words it uses | What it detects |
|---|---|---|---|
| **INSP-1** | **Serial** | "Read the serial number on the label. Tap the number that matches." The app shows the expected serial and two decoys | **Substitution.** The only check that does |
| **INSP-2** | **Seal** | "Is the silver seal across the base whole, and does it read `<code>`?" | Case opening; a device that has been apart and put back together |
| **INSP-3** | **Surface** | "Look at the card slot, the tap area and the keypad. Is anything **added, glued, raised or a different colour**?" | **Overlay skimmer.** The check the whole regime exists for |
| **INSP-4** | **Cable** | "Follow the cable from the device to where it goes in. Is there **one cable**, and is there **nothing extra plugged in anywhere along it**?" | Inline capture devices, unauthorised peripherals |
| **INSP-5** | **Fit** | "Pick the device up. Does it sit right in the stand, does it feel the way it usually does, and does the screen show your store number?" | Substitution the serial check might miss; a device that has been swapped in its housing |
| — | **Photograph** | "Take one photo of the front and one of the base." Compared side by side against the reference image captured at deployment | The evidence artefact, and the thing that makes a fast pass detectable |

INSP-5 is the check that reads as unscientific and is the one field colleagues rate most useful. **The person who works that lane every day is the best-calibrated sensor Marketa has for that lane**, and "does it feel the way it usually does" is the only question in the set that uses that calibration. Three of the six Q3 discrepancies in §8 were raised on INSP-5 before the colleague had read the seal code.

### 6.3 The inspection aid

A laminated card lives at every lane, behind the terminal, and it is deliberately not a copy of the app. It carries **photographs** — this is a normal card slot, this is a slot with an overlay; this is a normal seal, this is a lifted one; this is what one cable looks like, this is what two look like — and one sentence at the bottom: *If anything looks different, stop using the terminal and press REPORT. Nobody gets in trouble for a false alarm.*

The card was written by Store Operations and rewritten twice after being tested on colleagues who had not seen it before. The first version used the word "tamper". The second used "compromise". **The third uses "added, glued, raised or a different colour", and comprehension in the pilot went from 61% to 94%.** That is not a communications anecdote; it is the difference between a control that operates and one that is documented.

### 7.2 The repair-personnel rule — POI-5

The rule is one sentence and it is on the aid card: **nobody touches a terminal without an open work order the colleague can read, naming the engineer standing in front of them.**

| Step | What the colleague does | What the app does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Asks the engineer for the work-order number and photographic identity | — |

### 7.3 The unannounced verification tests, and the two that failed

Store Operations ran **nine unannounced tests** across the estate in the twelve months to 2026-06-30. In each, a Marketa colleague unknown to the store presented as a Verition field engineer, in plausible clothing, with a plausible reason, and **no work order**, and asked to be shown to a terminal.

| Outcome | Count | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Refused correctly** | **7** | The colleague asked for a work-order number, entered it or found none, and declined. Four escalated to the store manager; three pressed REPORT unprompted |
| **Not refused** | **2** | Access to the counter area was granted before verification. In both cases the store manager was off site and the shift lead had joined **within the previous five weeks** |

Two failures in nine is a 22% failure rate on the most likely real-world attack vector, and it is reported at the front of this section rather than buried at the back of it. Both failures fell on the same profile: a new colleague, without a manager present, facing a confident adult who said they were from the vendor. **That is not a training gap in the individual. It is the predictable result of asking a five-week employee to refuse an authority figure**, and the corrective actions are designed on that basis rather than on the basis that the colleagues concerned should have known better.

| Corrective action | Owner | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Both stores re-briefed; neither colleague named in any record beyond the immediate coaching note | Adaeze Nwosu | 2026-05-06 |
| The **red banner** added to the app so the refusal is the system's answer and not the colleague's | Adaeze Nwosu with Trevor Kim | 2026-05-22 |
| Script added to the aid card — *"I need to check the work order first, it will take a second"* — because refusing is easier with words to use | Adaeze Nwosu | 2026-05-22 |
| Verition asked to have every dispatched engineer quote the work-order number **before** entering the store, and to carry it on the dispatch notification | Adaeze Nwosu | 2026-06-03 |
| **Test programme expanded from 9 a year to 48 — one per region per quarter**, with results reported to the monthly store readiness call | Adaeze Nwosu | **OI-05-05**, due 2026-09-30 |

### 8.3 The observer effect, stated

**Q3 is the only cycle at 100% coverage, and that is not a coincidence.** Q3 ended in October, four weeks before Sable Ridge arrived on site, and Store Operations pushed the last 25 terminals to completion in a way it had not in Q1 or Q2. The programme records this rather than presenting the 100% as the regime's natural performance, for two reasons. It is true. And a coverage figure that rises in the quarter before an assessment and falls afterwards is a finding about the regime, not a credit to it — **the number to watch is Q1 of the 2027 trading year**, and it is scheduled to be reported to the Steering Committee against the Q3 figure specifically.

### 8.4 The six Q3 seal discrepancies, and why the procedure changed anyway

Six terminals in six different stores were found in the Q3 cycle with a tamper-evident seal that did not match what the app expected — three broken, two present but bearing a code the register did not hold, one intact but visibly lifted at a corner. Every one was escalated under POI-8 within two hours, removed from service and replaced from the spare pool. Every one was investigated by Store Operations with Verition. **All six were cleared as maintenance.** Five were resealed devices from the September battery and firmware service wave whose new seal codes had not been written back to the register; one had a seal degraded by a solvent a cleaning contractor used on the counter.

The instinct at that point is to close the six, note that the process worked, and move on. The programme did not, and the reason is the most transferable thing in this document.

> **"All cleared" is not the same as "nothing to learn".**
>
> Six escalations produced six correct answers and one uncomfortable fact: **five of the six were caused by Marketa's own service process, and the sixth by a contractor Marketa's stores let in.** The inspection did not detect an attack. It detected that the register's seal data was wrong on at least five devices, and it did so by accident, in the course of looking for something else. Had a real overlay been present on a seventh terminal in the same cycle, it would have been triaged by a store team that had already seen five identical-looking discrepancies turn out to be nothing — and the sixth false positive is what makes the seventh event dangerous.

| Change made | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **A reseal is now a register event.** Any Verition service action that breaks or replaces a seal must write the new seal code to the register through the same hard-gated flow as a device swap, before the device returns to service | Removes the cause of five of the six. The register was wrong; the seal was not |
| **Verition service waves are pre-notified to Store Operations with a device list**, and affected terminals are flagged in the app for the next inspection | A colleague inspecting a device serviced last week should know that, and should still escalate — but the triage should not start from zero |
| **The escalation path stays unchanged.** A seal discrepancy remains a suspected tamper, remains a POI-8 escalation, and the terminal still comes out of service | Deliberately unchanged. The temptation after six false positives is to downgrade the default disposition, and that is exactly the change that converts a working control into a paper one |
| **Cleaning contractors are named in the store physical standard**, with an approved product list and a prohibition on solvents at the lane | The sixth cause, and a genuine one |
| A **quarterly seal-code reconciliation** between the register and Verition's service record, independent of the inspection | The inspection found this by accident. A control should not depend on that twice |

The middle row is the one the Steering Committee spent the most time on and the one worth stating explicitly. **Six false positives is an argument for fixing the cause of the false positives. It is never an argument for raising the threshold at which the estate takes a seal discrepancy seriously.**

## 9. The Hardest Problem

Everything above describes a control. This section describes why it is difficult, and it is written plainly because the difficulty is the interesting part and because a document that omitted it would be describing a different company.

### 9.1 The people who operate this control

**Approximately 29,600 store colleagues perform 9.5.1.2.** They are not security professionals. They did not choose this task, it is not what they are measured on, and for most of them it appears four times a year in an app between two customers. Retail store turnover is high in normal months and the estate takes on **up to 6,800 temporary colleagues between October and January** — which means that in Q3 and Q4, the cycles covering peak trading, a meaningful share of the inspection workforce joined within the last few weeks. **CON-09** states the constraint; **R-13** scores it; **05.11** treats it.

No amount of policy changes any of that. What can be changed is the task.

| Design choice | Rejected alternative | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Five checks** | A fifteen-point checklist covering every documented indicator | A fifteen-point list is more thorough on paper and is completed less honestly. Five is the number a colleague can hold in their head, and the five chosen are the ones that detect the two attacks nothing else detects |
| **Ninety seconds** | A thorough five-minute inspection | Five minutes a terminal is thirty-three hours per cycle per region and it will not happen during trading. A task that cannot be done during a shift will be done at the end of one, from memory, at a desk |
| **On the handheld they already carry** | A dedicated inspection device or a web form on the back-office PC | The back office is not where the terminal is. Any design that separates the inspection from the lane produces inspections performed away from the lane |
| **Photographs, compared to a reference image** | A tick box | The photograph is the artefact, the quality signal and the deterrent. A tick box records that somebody tapped a screen |
| **Plain words — "added, glued, raised or a different colour"** | "Evidence of tampering or substitution" | Comprehension in the pilot: 61% against 94%. The requirement's vocabulary is for the assessor. The task's vocabulary is for the colleague |
| **No consequence for a false alarm, stated on the aid card** | A quality metric on unnecessary escalations | The moment a colleague believes a wrong report costs them something, the report rate goes to zero and stays there, and the estate loses its only overlay sensor between cycles |

### 9.2 Completion is not quality

The easiest thing to measure is whether an inspection record exists. It is also nearly worthless on its own: a colleague can complete 100% of the estate in fifteen minutes by tapping through. Marketa measures three proxies for whether the inspection happened as well as being recorded.

| Proxy | Mechanism | Result in the year |
|---|---|---|
| **Dwell time per terminal** | The app records time between opening and submitting the record. Median **96 seconds**. Any record under **25 seconds** is auto-flagged and the terminal is re-inspected by a second colleague | **341 flagged.** 338 re-inspected clean; **3 found a discrepancy the fast pass had missed** — two wear findings and one cable finding, all in Q1 |
| **Photograph quality** | Automated rejection of images that are blurred, dark, or do not resolve the seal area; the record cannot be submitted until an acceptable image is captured | **1,204 rejections** across 7,622 inspections — 15.8%. Rejection rate fell from 24% in Q1 to 11% in Q4 as colleagues learned what the app would accept |
| **Unannounced verification tests** | §7.3 | **9 tests, 7 passes, 2 failures** — the most useful and least flattering measure in this document |

The three flagged-and-then-found discrepancies are the reason the dwell-time rule survives despite generating 341 re-inspections for 3 findings. A control that catches one fast pass in a hundred is not efficient. **It is the only mechanism Marketa has that distinguishes an inspection from a tap**, and its real output is the 338 that came back clean, because those colleagues now know the app is watching.

### 9.3 What Marketa does not claim about the people

The programme does not claim that 29,600 people perform this control to a uniform standard, because they demonstrably do not — two of nine stores failed the repair-personnel test, 341 records were completed in under 25 seconds, and 15.8% of photographs had to be retaken. What it claims is narrower and defensible: **the task is simple enough to be performed correctly by someone who joined six weeks ago, the evidence it produces is attributable and dated, the quality of its performance is measured rather than assumed, and the failures it produces are visible.**

There is also a claim the programme makes in the other direction, which the operating data supports. **The most likely finder of a real overlay is not the quarterly inspector. It is the colleague who works that lane every day and notices the device feels different.** That is why POI-7 exists, why it is one button, why it is genuinely consequence-free and why the 118 colleague reports in the year — of which 77 found no fault at all — are reported as a success rather than as noise. Seventy-seven no-fault reports is seventy-seven occasions on which somebody looked at a payment terminal and thought about it. The regime is designed to buy that, and the quarterly cycle is partly a mechanism for keeping the possibility in mind for the other eighty-nine days.

## 11. Position at 2026-07-15, and What This Document Does Not Claim

**9.5.1 — In Place.** Devices protected from tampering and substitution by inventory, custody control, inspection, training and escalation, with the P2PE tamper-response and telemetry layer beneath. **9.5.1.1 — In Place**, 1,914 devices with make, model, location and two independently sourced serial numbers, reconciled monthly to telemetry and quarterly to a human reading a label, plus 74 spares. **9.5.1.2 — In Place**, four cycles, 7,622 inspections, 99.56% coverage, 0 terminals uninspected across the year. **9.5.1.2.1 — In Place**, determination made and approved under **TRA-9.5.1.2.1** at quarterly full visual and serial verification plus the POI-4 event triggers, with a stated review date and seven update triggers. **9.5.1.3 — In Place**, 97.6% blended completion, a repair-personnel rule with a system-generated refusal, and a consequence-free reporting channel.

| Claim | Position |
|---|---|
| That zero confirmed tampering proves the regime works | It does not. **Zero findings is consistent with a clean estate and with a regime that would not have found one.** The evidence that distinguishes them is the quality data in §9.2 and the verification tests in §7.3, and the verification tests failed twice in nine |
| That 99.56% coverage is the regime's steady-state performance | Q3 reached 100% four weeks before QSA fieldwork and no other cycle did. §8.3 says so. The number that will tell the truth is Q1 of the 2027 trading year |
| That the six Q3 discrepancies were a success for the control | They were a success for the escalation path and a **failure of the register**, five of six caused by Marketa's own service process. The procedure changed because of the cause, not because of the outcome |
| That the seven Phase 02 serial mismatches are fully evidenced | Five have an RMA record. **Two were reconstructed after the fact** from a job record and a store maintenance log, and are recorded as reconstructed. All seven were confirmed by Verition as supplied and key-injected under the listed solution, which is the substantive assurance and is not the same as a contemporaneous custody record |
| That 29,600 colleagues perform this control uniformly | They do not. 341 records were submitted in under 25 seconds, 15.8% of photographs were rejected, and two of nine stores admitted an unverified engineer. **All three numbers are reported because the alternative is a control whose failure modes are invisible** |
| That the inspection is Marketa's main defence against POI compromise | For internal tamper it is not — the device is. For an overlay or a substitution it is the **only** defence, and that is why the five checks are the five they are |
| That P2PE reduces the importance of this requirement | It inverts it. The scope reduction removing 482 servers and 1,914 lanes from the CDE is conditional on **P2PE-C7**, which is this document. A weaker 9.5.1 regime is not only a Requirement 9 finding; it is a threat to the largest scope reduction in the programme |
