## Purpose

Requirement **11.3.2** — external vulnerability scans performed by a PCI SSC **Approved Scanning Vendor** at least once every three months, with passing results — and **11.3.2.1**, external scans after any significant change.

| Sub-requirement | Obligation |
|---|---|
| **11.3.2** | External vulnerability scans **at least once every three months and after any significant change**; performed by a **PCI SSC Approved Scanning Vendor**; vulnerabilities resolved and the **ASV Program Guide requirements for a passing scan** met; rescans performed as needed |
| **11.3.2.1** | External scans performed **after any significant change**; vulnerabilities ranked high-risk or critical under 6.3.1 resolved; rescans as needed; scans performed by qualified personnel with organisational independence — **an ASV is not required for this scan** |

This is the only requirement in PCI DSS that obliges a merchant to obtain a specific artefact from a specifically qualified external party on a fixed cadence, and to send it to somebody else. It is therefore the requirement with the least room to manoeuvre and the most visible failure mode: **the acquirer knows the result whether or not the entity reports it.**

**Marketa's year: four quarterly scans. Q1 failed with three findings and was rescanned clean in eleven days. Q2, Q3 and Q4 passed first time.**

## 1. The ASV, and the Independence Position

Marketa's ASV is **Sable Ridge Scanning Services**. Marketa's QSA is **Sable Ridge Assurance, LLC**. They are the same firm, operating as separate practices, and Council rules permit a single firm to hold both qualifications and serve the same client.

**ADR-0003** records the decision to engage both and to document the independence position rather than leave it implicit. The reasoning is worth restating here because this document is where the arrangement is tested.

| Element | Position |
|---|---|
| The threat | Not that the firm is dishonest. **That the arrangement is never examined.** An unexamined convenience is a finding waiting for somebody else to make it |
| Separate personnel and reporting lines | Confirmed in both engagement letters |
| Advisory work | **Expressly excluded** from both engagements. The ASV may not tell Marketa how to fix what it found, beyond the remediation guidance the ASV Program Guide obliges it to include in the report |
| **The structural safeguard** | **The Attestation of Scan Compliance is issued to Cardinal Merchant Bank independently of Marketa.** Marketa does not stand between the ASV and the acquirer, and cannot choose which results are transmitted |
| Familiarity threat | Assessor rotation reviewed at the **2028** cycle |
| Internal Audit view | Rosa Delgado reviews the arrangement annually and reports to the Audit Committee |

### 1.1 The Q1 failure is the evidence

The sceptical question about a same-firm QSA and ASV arrangement is precise: *could quarterly scan findings be handled gently because the same firm is assessing the Report on Compliance?*

The answer to that question is not an assurance. It is a scan that failed.

> **In Q1 2026, Sable Ridge Scanning Services issued a failing scan against Marketa's external footprint. The failure was reported to Cardinal Merchant Bank as a failure. It was rescanned clean eleven days later, and the failing result remains on the record.**

A same-firm arrangement that had softened findings would have produced four clean quarters and no evidence of anything. **The failure is the structural proof that the arrangement works**, and it is reported first in this document rather than buried in the middle, because an entity that treats a failed scan as an embarrassment is an entity whose next failed scan will be handled differently.

## 2. The Scan Scope

An ASV scan covers all internet-accessible system components in the cardholder data environment and any internet-accessible components that could provide a path to it. Defining that population correctly is the entity's obligation, and getting it wrong is the most common cause of an ASV programme that looks compliant and is not.

| Population | Count | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Internet-facing IP addresses in scan scope | **58** | Storefront edge and origin, payment-page delivery origins, the corporate perimeter, the remote-access concentrator, mail gateways, and the CDE-05 external file-transfer interfaces |
| Fully qualified domain names in scan scope | **31** | Including every hostname resolving to a payment page or to a page that embeds the Truvance iframe |
| Load-balanced or CDN-fronted services requiring the ASV's specific handling | 9 | Declared to the ASV so that the scan reaches origins rather than only edge nodes |
| Actively blocked by the perimeter during scanning | **0** | §2.1 |
| Components in scope for 11.3.1 internal scanning and **not** in ASV scope | 62 of the 71 | Internal by definition; covered in 06.05 |

Scope is reconciled quarterly, before each scan, against four sources: the external DNS zone data, the perimeter address allocations, the cloud public-address inventory, and the CDN and edge configuration. **Four sources, for the same reason the component register uses four** — the failure in Q1 was a host that appeared in one of them and in nothing else.

### 2.1 Not interfering with the scan

The ASV Program Guide obliges the scan customer not to configure its environment to block or filter the ASV's scanning activity, and obliges the ASV to detect and report evidence of active blocking. This is a genuine obligation with a genuine temptation attached: a web application firewall in aggressive mode will make a scan look clean by refusing to let the scanner see anything.

Marketa's arrangement: the ASV's source ranges are **neither allow-listed nor blocked or rate-limited** — they are treated as ordinary internet traffic. The web application firewall and the perimeter intrusion prevention are left in their production configuration, with no rule relaxed and none tightened for the scan window; scan traffic generates alerts, which are pre-notified to the on-call responder and adjudicated normally. The ASV's report confirms no active blocking in all four quarters, and Marketa retains its own perimeter logs for each scan window and reconciles them against the ASV's source addresses.

Both halves matter. **An entity that whitelists the ASV is scanning a configuration no attacker sees, and an entity that blocks it is buying a clean report.**

## 3. What a Passing Scan Means

"Passing" is a defined term in the ASV Program Guide, not a judgement. Understanding it is what separates an entity that remediates the right things from one that argues about scores.

| Condition for failure | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Any vulnerability with a CVSS base score of 4.0 or higher** | The threshold is not "High". A Medium-severity finding at 4.3 fails an ASV scan. This surprises entities whose internal ranking treats 4.3 as routine |
| **Automatic-failure conditions regardless of CVSS score** | Including SQL injection, cross-site scripting, directory traversal, remote code execution, insecure SSL/TLS configuration, and the presence of unpatched software the vendor no longer supports. **A score of 0.0 does not save a finding in this class** |
| Findings the ASV determines are exceptions or false positives | May be disputed by the scan customer **with evidence**, and the ASV determines the outcome. A disputed finding is not a removed finding until the ASV agrees |
| Informational findings below 4.0 and outside the automatic-failure classes | Do not cause failure. **Marketa remediates them anyway under the TRA-11.3.1.1 bands**, because a finding the ASV can see from the internet is a finding an attacker can see from the internet |

### 3.1 Disputes

The ASV Program Guide permits the scan customer to dispute findings with supporting evidence. Across four quarters, **Marketa raised zero disputes.**

That is reported with a caveat rather than as a boast. Zero disputes can mean the scanning was accurate. It can also mean nobody read the findings closely enough to challenge them. Marketa's basis for the first reading: every finding in Q1 was independently reproduced by Marketa's own team before remediation began, and the informational findings in Q2 to Q4 were each triaged and dispositioned under the 11.3.1.1 bands rather than being filed unread. **The evidence that findings were read is the remediation of the ones that did not have to be remediated.**

### 4.2 The root cause

Two of the three findings existed on a host that had been provisioned eleven days before the scan to serve a seasonal marketing campaign. A subdomain was repointed to it. The host was built from a stale image, it terminated TLS with a deprecated protocol version enabled, and it ran a web server release two versions behind.

**It had not been through a scope-delta review.** It appeared in the external DNS zone data and in nothing else — not the CMDB, not the perimeter address allocation, not the cloud public-address inventory. Under the four-source reconciliation it would have been caught at the next quarterly scope reconciliation, which was scheduled after the scan.

| Consequence | Action |
|---|---|
| The scope-delta trigger did not fire because a DNS change was not classified as scope-affecting | **A public DNS record change is now a scope-affecting change** under the 12.5.2 process, with the same trigger obligations as a network or integration change |
| The host was built from a stale image | Marketing campaign infrastructure moved onto the standard edge build with the Phase 04 baselines; the stale image withdrawn |
| Scope reconciliation ran quarterly, after the scan | Reconciliation moved to **immediately before** each scan, and the DNS zone data is now diffed daily with changes alerted |
| The finding pattern | Recorded against **R-33** — scope creep, a new system entering the estate without a scope determination. The register entry is not reduced by this remediation; the instance is closed and the class is not |

The third finding — the reverse-proxy patch — was an ordinary miss and is recorded as one. It had been released 34 days before the scan against a 30-day SLA, and it appears in the 04.06 patching exception record.

### 4.3 What the failure demonstrates, and what it does not

| Reading | Position |
|---|---|
| That the ASV arrangement is independent | **Supported.** A failing scan was issued and transmitted to the acquirer by a firm that also assesses Marketa's ROC |
| That Marketa's external attack surface was well managed in February | **Not supported.** A host with a deprecated TLS configuration and an outdated web server was internet-facing for eleven days, and the reason nobody knew is that a DNS change was not treated as a scope event |
| That the failure was well handled | Supported. Reproduced within a day, acquirer notified ahead of the attestation, remediated in a week, root cause established before the rescan rather than after it |
| That 11.3.2 was breached | **No.** The requirement obliges passing results and rescans as needed; the ASV Program Guide contemplates rescanning until a passing scan is achieved. Four passing quarterly scans exist within the twelve months. **The failure is not a compliance breach and is not presented as one — nor is it presented as nothing** |

The second row is the honest one. It is tempting to describe the Q1 failure purely as a governance success because of how it was handled. It was also a control failure, and the control that failed was scope management, not scanning.

## 5. External Scanning After Significant Change — 11.3.2.1

11.3.2.1 obliges external scans after any significant change; high-risk and critical vulnerabilities resolved; rescans as needed; and scans performed by qualified personnel with organisational independence — **which need not be an ASV or a QSA.**

The distinction between 11.3.2 and 11.3.2.1 is frequently missed in both directions. Some entities engage the ASV for every change, which is expensive and unnecessary. Others treat the quarterly ASV scan as discharging the change obligation, which leaves an internet-facing change unexamined for up to three months.

| | **11.3.2** | **11.3.2.1** |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | At least every three months | **After any significant change** |
| Who performs it | **Must be a PCI SSC Approved Scanning Vendor** | Qualified personnel with organisational independence. **Internal is permitted** |
| Standard applied | ASV Program Guide passing-scan criteria — the 4.0 threshold and the automatic-failure classes | High-risk and critical vulnerabilities **as ranked under 6.3.1** resolved |
| Artefact | Attestation of Scan Compliance | Marketa's own scan report, retained as evidence |
| Marketa's arrangement | Sable Ridge Scanning Services, four cycles | Marketa security operations, using CT-31 from an external vantage point |

| Change-driven external scan in the period | Count | Findings | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| New or re-provisioned internet-facing host | 6 | 2 — a Medium and a Low on one host | Resolved before public resolution was enabled; rescanned |
| Change to the payment-page delivery or CDN configuration | 3 | 0 | — |
| Perimeter rule-set change affecting an exposed service | 4 | 1 High — an administrative interface exposed by a rule error | Resolved in 3 hours; rescanned clean the same day |
| Certificate or TLS configuration change on an exposed service | 2 | 0 | — |
| **Total** | **15** | **3** | **All resolved with a confirming rescan** |

Marketa applies its own passing-scan standard to these scans rather than the lower bar 11.3.2.1 sets. **The requirement obliges only high-risk and critical findings to be resolved; Marketa applies the ASV Program Guide criteria**, so that a change-driven scan cannot pass with a finding that would fail the next quarterly ASV scan. The alternative arrangement produces a change that is compliant on Tuesday and fails the quarterly scan in May, which is a way of scheduling a failure rather than avoiding one.

Since the Q1 root cause, **a public DNS record change is a significant change** for this purpose, which is what converted the change-driven scan population from 11 to 15 in the year.

## 6. The Attestation of Scan Compliance

The Attestation of Scan Compliance is the artefact 11.3.2 actually produces, and it is more than a certificate.

| Component | Content | Who signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Attestation of Scan Compliance** | The scan customer's declaration of the scope of the scan and its completeness; the ASV's declaration of the scan result | **Both** — Marketa and Sable Ridge Scanning Services |
| **ASV Scan Report Summary** | The components scanned, the number of vulnerabilities by severity, and the compliance status | ASV |
| **ASV Scan Vulnerability Details** | Every finding, with its CVSS score, its status, and remediation guidance | ASV |

The scan-customer signature is the part entities sign without reading. **In signing, Marketa attests that the scope submitted to the ASV was complete** — that no internet-facing component in or connected to the cardholder data environment was omitted. That attestation is why the four-source scope reconciliation in §2 exists, and why the Q1 root cause was treated as a scope failure rather than as a patching failure. Signing an attestation of completeness for a scope produced by a reconciliation that runs after the scan would be signing something Marketa could not know.

| Attestation | Scope declared | Signed by | Transmitted to Cardinal | Within the C4 window |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 58 addresses, 31 hostnames | Owen Castellanos; Sable Ridge Scanning Services | 2026-03-06 | Yes |
| Q2 | 58 addresses, 31 hostnames | Owen Castellanos; Sable Ridge Scanning Services | 2026-05-28 | Yes |
| Q3 | 57 addresses, 31 hostnames | Owen Castellanos; Sable Ridge Scanning Services | 2026-08-27 | Yes |
| Q4 | 57 addresses, 32 hostnames | Owen Castellanos; Sable Ridge Scanning Services | 2026-11-26 | Yes |

The address count falls by one after Q2 — the campaign host from the Q1 root cause, decommissioned rather than rebuilt in place, with a data-destruction attestation under procedure P-3. The hostname count rises by one in Q4 as a seasonal storefront hostname enters scope, this time through the scope-delta process.

## 8. Risks Treated

| Risk | Rating | How this document treats it | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R-29** — an ASV scan fails and remediation plus rescan misses the 30-day attestation window under obligation C4 | Low 6 | **The risk materialised in Q1 and the window was met with 19 days to spare.** The Critical SLA of 7 days on internet-exploitable findings, and the ranking rule that makes anything internet-exploitable Critical, are both sized for exactly this | Low, and now evidenced rather than assumed |
| **R-33** — scope creep: a new system enters the estate without a scope determination | Moderate 12 | The Q1 root cause is an instance of this class. DNS record changes are now scope-affecting; zone data is diffed daily; scope reconciliation moved ahead of the scan | **Moderate.** The instance is closed; the class is not |
| **R-35** — an application flaw in the storefront exposes order data, tokens or customer records | Low 6 | Quarterly external scanning plus change-driven scanning at the ASV standard; the application penetration test in 06.08 | Low |
| **R-09** — third-party service provider concentration | High 15 | Not treated here. The payment page's own integrity is 6.4.3 and 11.6.1; the provider assurance position is 06.10 | High |
| **R-01** — unauthorised script on a payment page | High 20 | **An ASV scan does not detect a script injected into a page served correctly by a healthy host.** Recorded here so that a passing external scan is not misread as evidence about payment-page integrity. That control is **11.6.1 in 06.09** | High |

The final row is the most important sentence in this document for a reader deciding what a passing ASV scan proves. **It proves that the external attack surface presented no failing vulnerability on the scan date.** It says nothing about a third-party script loaded at run time into a page that the scan found perfectly healthy, and Marketa's highest-rated risk is exactly that.
