## Purpose

Requirement **10.3** — protection of audit logs — and **10.5.1** — retention of audit log history. Four protection sub-requirements and one retention sub-requirement, and between them they decide whether the record produced in 06.01 is worth anything.

The threat model is short. An adversary who reaches a system with the intent to persist does three things to the audit record, in this order: **stops it, alters it, deletes it**. 10.2.1.6 makes the first visible. 10.3.2 and 10.3.4 make the second visible. 10.3.3 makes the third ineffective, because the copy that matters is already somewhere else. 10.3.1 addresses a different and quieter threat — the reader who should not be reading, whose interest in the audit record is the record itself.

| Sub-requirement | Obligation |
|---|---|
| **10.3.1** | Read access to audit log files is limited to those with a job-related need |
| **10.3.2** | Audit log files are protected to prevent modifications by individuals |
| **10.3.3** | Audit log files, **including those for external-facing technologies**, are promptly backed up to a secure, central, internal log server or other media that is difficult to modify |
| **10.3.4** | File integrity monitoring or change-detection mechanisms are used on audit logs, so that existing log data cannot be changed without generating alerts |
| **10.5.1** | Audit log history retained for **at least 12 months**, with **at least the most recent 3 months immediately available** for analysis |

## 1. The Retention Architecture

Marketa holds audit history in three tiers. The tier boundaries are set by what an analyst or an assessor needs to do with the data, not by what the storage costs.

| Tier | Window | Location | Medium | Query behaviour | Serves |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hot** | **0 to 6 months** | CT-19, Columbus, replicated to Ashburn | Indexed online storage | Interactive search; median result in **under 4 seconds** for a single-component 30-day query | The 10.5.1 "immediately available" obligation, with a **6-month** window against a 3-month floor |
| **Warm** | **6 to 15 months** | CT-19 archive tier, Columbus and Ashburn | Object storage with an index catalogue | Search by rehydration; median **11 minutes** to a searchable set | The 10.5.1 12-month obligation, with 3 months of margin |
| **Cold** | **15 months to 7 years** | Ashburn and a third-party archive | Write-once object storage, separate credential domain | Restoration by request under LOG-P5; measured at **under 6 hours** | Legal hold, dispute reconstruction, forensic reach-back. **Not a PCI obligation** |

### 1.1 Why 15 months rather than 12

The obligation is twelve months. Marketa retains **fifteen**, and the reason is arithmetic rather than caution.

A rolling twelve-month retention implemented against a twelve-month obligation is compliant on the day it is configured and non-compliant on any day the deletion job runs early, the clock drifts, a time zone is misapplied at a tier boundary, or an ingest backlog shifts a record's effective age. Every one of those is a real failure mode and none of them is exotic. **Three months of margin means the retention control can fail silently for a quarter without breaching the requirement**, which converts a compliance failure into an operational finding with time to fix it.

The same reasoning sets the hot tier at six months rather than three. The 10.5.1 floor is three months immediately available; an incident discovered in month four is not unusual, and an analyst who has to rehydrate to answer the first question of an investigation loses the hour in which the answer would have been useful.

### 1.2 What is retained, and for how long, by class

| Log class | Hot | Warm | Cold | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Audit logs of the 71 assessed components | 6 months | to 15 months | 7 years | 10.5.1 plus dispute reconstruction |
| Store server and network audit logs; privileged session recordings; change-detection events; wireless controller and rogue classification | 6 months | to 15 months | 3 years | 10.5.1; 10.2.1.2; 10.3.4 and 11.5.2; 11.2.1 quarterly evidence |
| **SIEM and archive access records — the 10.2.1.3 index** | **15 months hot** | — | 7 years | Never tiered down. The index that records who read the logs is the one an investigation reaches for last and needs most |
| ASV and internal scan reports | Held as documents | — | 7 years | 11.3; the acquirer's attestation history |

The third row is a deliberate asymmetry. Everything else ages out of the hot tier at six months. The record of **who searched the logs** stays hot for fifteen, because the question it answers — was somebody looking at this before the incident — is asked only after an incident and is asked about a period well before it.

## 2. Read Access to Audit Logs — 10.3.1

10.3.1 obliges read access to audit log files to be limited to those with a job-related need. It is a Requirement 7 control applied to a Requirement 10 asset, and the discipline is the same: the population is defined, enumerated, reviewed and small.

| Role | Individuals | What they can read | Job-related need |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security operations analyst | **9** | All log classes except the 10.2.1.3 access index | Daily review and adjudication under 10.4.1 |
| Security Operations Manager | 1 — Marcus Hale | All classes including the access index | Control ownership |
| Incident responder, on activation | **4**, time-bounded | All classes for the scope and window of the incident | 12.10 response; access granted per activation and expires with it |
| Northbridge after-hours analyst | **6 named individuals** | Alert context and the log classes named in the 12.8.5 matrix. **No access to the archive tier, no export capability** | After-hours triage |
| Internal Audit — Rosa Delgado's team | 3 | All classes including the access index; **read-only, cannot be revoked by security operations** | Independent review; the assurance role cannot depend on the audited function's goodwill |
| Platform engineer | **11** | Operational telemetry for their own platform only. **Not the audit stream** | Troubleshooting |
| Assessor — Sable Ridge | 2, engagement-bounded | All classes, read-only, for the assessment window | ROC evidence |
| **Total with any audit-log read access** | **36** | | |

Three properties of that table are load-bearing. **Platform engineers are separated from the audit stream** — an engineer troubleshooting a database needs performance telemetry, not the record of who queried cardholder data, and conflating the two is how the read population reaches three hundred people. **Northbridge cannot export**, so an outsourced watch can see what it needs to triage and cannot take a copy of Marketa's audit history off Marketa's platform. And **Internal Audit's access cannot be revoked by security operations**, because a control whose evidence the controlled party can withdraw is not independently reviewable.

The 36 are reviewed in the 7.2.4 cycle alongside every other entitlement, and the 05.03 Cycle 2 review produced **one** revocation here: a platform engineer who had retained analyst-level access after a role change in 2025.

## 3. Protection from Modification — 10.3.2

10.3.2 obliges audit log files to be protected to prevent modification by individuals. "Prevent" is stronger than "detect", and the architecture is built to prevent first and detect second, because detection alone means the modification has already happened.

| Layer | Mechanism | Prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **Write-only egress from the CDE** | CT-20 forwarders may write outward to CT-18 and cannot be reached inward. A compromised CDE component cannot reach the collector at all | Modification of already-forwarded records from the source system |
| **No update path in the platform** | CT-18's ingest is append-only by design. There is no supported operation that edits a stored event. Deletion exists only as a tier-lifecycle action executed by the platform, not by a person | Editing of stored events |
| **Object-lock immutability on the archive** | The warm and cold tiers are held under an object-lock retention policy in compliance mode. **No credential in Marketa's possession — including the root of the storage account — can shorten the lock or delete a locked object before its expiry** | Deletion or replacement of archived records |
| **Separated credential domains** | The archive storage account is in a separate cloud account with a separate identity domain. A compromise of the Marketa production identity plane does not yield the archive | Administrative deletion following a domain compromise |
| **Dual control on lifecycle change** | Any change to a retention policy, a lock configuration or a tier boundary requires two named approvers and produces a 10.2.1.6 event | Quiet shortening of retention |
| **Cryptographic sealing** | Each archive segment is hashed on close; the hash chain is recorded in a separate ledger held on the CT-19 Ashburn side | Undetected substitution of a whole segment |

### 3.1 The immutability claim, stated precisely

Entities routinely claim immutable logs and mean "nobody normally deletes them". Marketa states what its arrangement actually guarantees and what it does not.

| Claim | True? | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| A stored event cannot be edited in place | **Yes** | The platform provides no update operation; storage is append-only |
| An archived object cannot be deleted before its retention expiry by any Marketa credential | **Yes** | Object lock in compliance mode; verified by an attempted deletion during the annual test — §6.2 |
| A record can never be prevented from arriving | **No** | A compromised source can stop emitting. That is 10.2.1.6 and the silence alert, not 10.3.2 |
| The hot tier is immutable in the same sense as the archive | **No** | The hot tier is append-only and access-controlled but is not object-locked. **The archive copy written within 60 seconds is the immutable one**, which is why 10.3.3's promptness matters |
| An administrator with full access cannot suppress future collection | **No** | They can, and the act is logged, alerted and independently visible through volume monitoring |

The fourth row is the reason 10.3.3 is a separate requirement rather than a detail of 10.3.2. Protection of the record depends on the record having been copied somewhere protected, promptly, and "promptly" is the whole of the control.

## 4. Prompt Central Backup — 10.3.3

10.3.3 obliges audit log files, **including those for external-facing technologies**, to be promptly backed up to a secure, central, internal log server or other media that is difficult to modify.

### 4.1 What "promptly" means at Marketa

| Population | Time from generation to immutable archive | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| CDE components — Z1 and Z2 | **Median 41 seconds**, 99th percentile 118 seconds | Continuous forwarding through CT-20; archive write on ingest |
| Connected-to components | Median 52 seconds | Direct forwarding |
| **External-facing technologies** — storefront edge, payment-page delivery, perimeter NSCs, the public DNS and CDN layer | **Median 38 seconds** | Streamed off the edge continuously; §4.2 |
| Store estate | Median 3 minutes 20 seconds; up to **72 hours** where a store circuit is down and the WAN edge is buffering | Store WAN edge buffer, drained on reconnection with original timestamps preserved |
| Vendor-hosted control planes | **Up to 5 minutes**, bounded by the provider API poll interval | Poll into CT-18, archived on ingest |

No component retains its audit record as the only copy for longer than its stated window. The store-estate row is the honest exception and it is bounded by design: a store that cannot reach the network holds up to 72 hours locally, and the loss of a store server during an outage would lose that window. It has not occurred; it is recorded as a known property rather than discovered later as a gap.

### 4.2 The external-facing limb

The phrase "including those for external-facing technologies" is in the requirement because external-facing systems are the ones an entity is most likely to treat as somebody else's logging problem — a content delivery network, a cloud load balancer, a managed web application firewall.

| External-facing technology | Log source | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Storefront edge and CDN | Provider real-time log stream | Streamed to CT-18 continuously; **not** left in the provider's console |
| Payment-page delivery tier — CT-52 to CT-57 | Access, origin and configuration-change events | Full retention; the configuration-change events feed 6.4.3 and, from 06.09, the 11.6.1 detective control |
| Web application firewall | Rule match, block, and rule-set change | Full retention |
| Perimeter network security controls — CT-43, CT-44 | Connection, deny, and configuration change | Full retention; configuration change treated as CDE-class |
| Public DNS | Zone change and delegation change | Full retention. A DNS change is how a payment page starts being served by somebody else |
| CDE-05 external file transfer interfaces | Session, authentication, transfer and integrity events | Full retention; the interface Cardinal and Truvance connect to |

The DNS row earned its place during the Q1 ASV cycle: two of the three Q1 findings existed because a marketing subdomain had been repointed to a newly provisioned edge host without a scope-delta review. The scan found the host. **The DNS change log is what established when it happened and who made the change**, and it is the reason 06.06 §4 can describe the root cause rather than the symptom.

## 5. File Integrity Monitoring on the Audit Logs — 10.3.4

10.3.4 obliges file integrity monitoring or change-detection mechanisms to be used **on audit logs**, so that existing log data cannot be changed without generating an alert.

This is a distinct requirement from 11.5.2, which applies change detection to critical files generally. The distinction is worth stating because the two are frequently satisfied with one deployment and then only one of them is evidenced.

| | **10.3.4** | **11.5.2** |
|---|---|---|
| Applies to | **Audit logs** | Critical files — system files, configuration files, content files |
| Purpose | Existing log data cannot be changed without an alert | Alert personnel to unauthorised modification of critical files |
| Comparison cadence | Not specified as a frequency; the obligation is that change generates an alert | **At least weekly** |
| Marketa's mechanism | CT-21 monitoring the log store, plus the hash-chain ledger in §3.1 | CT-21 across the 71 — delivered in **06.07** |

### 5.1 Marketa's implementation

| Layer | What it watches | Alert on |
|---|---|---|
| **CT-21 agent on the log store** | The CT-19 hot-tier file system, index metadata and configuration | Any modification, attribute change, permission change or deletion outside the platform's own lifecycle operations |
| **Hash chain over closed archive segments** | Each segment is hashed on close; the hash is written to a ledger held in the Ashburn side of CT-19 with a separate credential domain | Any divergence between a segment's computed hash and the ledger value |
| **Continuous chain verification** | A rolling verification job recomputes segment hashes across the full 15-month window on a **7-day cycle** | Divergence, or a segment present in the ledger and absent from storage, or the reverse |
| **Object-lock state assertion** | The lock configuration on every archive object is asserted daily against the expected policy | Any object whose lock has been shortened, removed or never applied |
| **On the source side** | The local audit files on CDE components, before forwarding | Truncation, unexpected rotation, permission change, or deletion of a local audit file |

The last row is the one that catches the realistic attack. An adversary on a CDE component cannot reach CT-18 and cannot alter what has already been forwarded — but they can truncate the local file, and if the only integrity monitoring is on the central store, that act is invisible until somebody compares the two. Marketa monitors both ends and reconciles them.

### 5.2 What the mechanism found in the period

| Event | Date | Classification | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Archive segment hash divergence on 2 segments | 2026-03-11 | **False positive** | A storage-tier migration rewrote object metadata; the payload hashes were unchanged. The verification job was corrected to hash payload only |
| Local audit file truncated on a connected-to component | 2026-04-28 | **True positive, benign cause** | A misconfigured third-party log-shipping agent installed by a platform team without going through LOG-P1. The agent was removed; LOG-P1 onboarding made a prerequisite in the change process |
| Permission change on the hot-tier index directory | 2026-06-02 | True positive, benign cause | A platform upgrade's installer widened a directory permission. Reverted within 40 minutes; the vendor's installer behaviour recorded against the baseline |
| Object-lock assertion failure on 1 object | 2026-06-19 | **True positive, control gap** | A newly created bucket prefix had not inherited the lock policy. **Nine hours of one log class was archived without a lock.** Corrected; the policy is now applied at the account level rather than at the prefix, and the daily assertion is what found it |

The 19 June entry is reported because it is a control gap rather than a false alarm. Nine hours of one log class sat in the archive without immutability, nobody touched it, and the daily assertion found it the next morning. An entity that reported only its false positives would look better and would be describing a monitoring programme that had never caught anything.

## 6. Retention and Immediate Availability — 10.5.1

10.5.1 obliges audit log history to be retained for **at least 12 months**, with **at least the most recent 3 months immediately available** for analysis.

### 6.1 Evidencing "immediately available"

The phrase is not defined in the standard, and that is precisely why it needs a defined and measured meaning. Marketa's position: **immediately available means an analyst can search it now, without a restoration request, a ticket, a rehydration wait or a third party.**

| Test | Method | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Interactive query across the full hot window | Single-component query spanning 6 months, executed monthly | Median **3.8 seconds**; slowest recorded **31 seconds** |
| Cross-component correlation query over 90 days | 8 CDE components, all event classes, executed monthly | Median **14 seconds** |
| Query at the 3-month boundary | A query whose window straddles the 3-month mark | Returns from the hot tier without rehydration — the boundary is at 6 months, not 3 |
| Query at the 12-month boundary | A query at 11 months 25 days | Returns from the warm tier by rehydration; median **11 minutes**. **Not claimed as immediately available**, and it does not need to be |
| Query during peak trading | The same correlation query executed during the November ramp at 96,000 events per second | Median **22 seconds** — degraded and still interactive |
| Availability of the hot tier | Measured over the operating period | **99.97%**; one 41-minute interruption during an index rebuild, recorded in the 10.7.2 failure register |

The fifth row is the one worth measuring. A logging platform that is interactive in April and unusable in November is immediately available exactly when nobody needs it. Marketa tests the query path under peak ingest because the incidents most likely to matter happen during the trading period the platform is least able to absorb.

### 6.2 The annual retention and restoration test

Retention is asserted by configuration and proved by retrieval. Once a year, and after any change to the storage architecture, Marketa executes a test that treats its own retention claim as a hypothesis.

| Step | Test | Result, 2026-06-15 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Retrieve a named event from **11 months and 20 days** ago on a CDE component | Retrieved in 9 minutes 40 seconds; all six 10.2.2 fields intact |
| 2 | Retrieve a full day of audit history for a store server from 13 months ago | Retrieved in 14 minutes; record count matched the ingest-day counter |
| 3 | Verify the hash chain across a randomly selected 30-day archive window | Verified; no divergence |
| 4 | **Attempt to delete a locked archive object** using the highest-privileged credential available | **Denied.** The denial event is the evidence for the §3.1 immutability claim |
| 5 | **Attempt to shorten the retention lock** on a locked object | **Denied** |
| 6 | Restore a cold-tier segment from 4 years ago | Restored in 3 hours 12 minutes, inside the 6-hour objective |
| 7 | Verify that the 10.2.1.3 access index for the same window is complete and separately governed | Verified; the deletion attempts in steps 4 and 5 appear in it |

Steps 4 and 5 are the substance of the test. An immutability claim that has never been tested by an attempt to violate it is a configuration screenshot, and the difference between a screenshot and a denial event is the difference between a described control and an evidenced one.

### 6.3 Retention against other obligations

| Obligation | Period | Interaction with 10.5.1 |
|---|---|---|
| Card-brand dispute and representment reconstruction | Up to 540 days on some dispute types | Satisfied by the 15-month warm tier and the 7-year cold tier |
| Call recording retention | **13 months** — 02.06 and R-24 | A separate estate with a separate rule. **Audit logs about the recording platform follow 10.5.1; the recordings themselves do not** |
| Legal hold | Indefinite, on instruction from the General Counsel | Suspends lifecycle deletion for the identified scope. **Three holds active at 2026-07-15**, none related to payment operations |
| Data minimisation under 3.2.1 | Retain no longer than necessary | Applies to account data, not to audit logs. **No audit log contains account data** — the collector quarantine rule in 06.01 §5.3 is what keeps that true |

The last row prevents a genuine confusion. Requirement 3.2.1 obliges account data not to be retained beyond business need; 10.5.1 obliges audit logs to be retained for twelve months. They do not conflict, and the only way they could is if audit logs contained account data — which is why the collector-side content rule exists and why the seven quarantined records in 06.01 §5.3 were treated as a 12.10.7 event rather than as a logging defect.
