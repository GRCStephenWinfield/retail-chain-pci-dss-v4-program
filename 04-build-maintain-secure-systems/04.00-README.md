| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Program | Marketa Retail Group — PCI DSS v4.0.1 Compliance Program |
| Phase | 04 — Build &amp; Maintain Secure Systems |
| Status | **Requirements 2–6 implemented · 3 unauthorised payment-page scripts found and removed · most risks HELD, not reduced** |
| Classification | Confidential — Cardholder Data Environment // Illustrative Portfolio Sample |

## What this phase is

PCI Requirements **2 through 6** built across the 71 assessed components: secure configuration, protecting stored account data, key management, transmission security, anti-malware, vulnerability management, secure development, change control, public-facing application protection, and — the one that matters most for a merchant with a hosted checkout iframe — **payment-page script control under 6.4.3**.

Requirement 1 was delivered in Phase 03 and is referenced, not re-delivered.

## The finding: three scripts nobody had authorised

The first inventory closed at **38 scripts across 6 payment templates, 11 of them third-party**. Three had no business justification, no owner, and no record of a decision to place them there. All three had arrived through the marketing tag manager — which **nineteen accounts** could use to publish to any template on the site, including the six where customers type their card number.

Nothing was compromised. The scripts were ordinary analytics pixels. **The tag manager was working exactly as designed.**

That is the point. As the E-Commerce Director put it at the March review:

> "The container has permission to execute arbitrary JavaScript on the page where our customers type their card number. It does not matter what we have put in it so far. It matters what it is capable of putting there, and who has to approve that. Right now the answer is nobody."

This is a **governance finding wearing a technology costume** — and it is a far more common shape of the Magecart problem than the dramatic one.

## What was done about it — ADR-0011

The obvious response was to govern the capability: approval workflow, alerts, quarterly container review. Marketa **removed the capability instead**. Payment-template publication no longer exists in the tag console; publication accounts went from 19 to 6, and none of the six can reach a payment template.

In exchange, Marketing received a **five-business-day approval SLA** through the engineering path. That SLA is load-bearing, not a courtesy — a control that makes the business wait indefinitely gets routed around, and the route will be less visible than the one just closed.

Eleven submissions followed: **seven approved, four refused.** Two of the three removed scripts were re-justified and conditionally re-authorised; the container was not reinstated.

## 6.4.3 is preventive. 11.6.1 is detective. Neither substitutes for the other.

| | **6.4.3** | **11.6.1** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Preventive** | **Detective** |
| Delivered | **This phase (04.10)** | **Phase 06** |
| What it does | Authorise each script, assure its integrity, inventory it with written justification | Detect and alert on unauthorised modification of payment-page content and HTTP headers |
| What it cannot do | Tell you an authorised third-party script has since been modified at its origin | Stop the modification — it tells you it happened |

The Steering Committee recorded explicitly that **the script control is not complete until both are operating.**

## Why almost nothing was reduced

Every control in this phase was deployed within the last three months. **A control with no operating history is a design, not a demonstrated reduction** — and the register moves when evidence accumulates, not when something is switched on.

The one clear reduction is **R-34 (payment-page script inventory drift)**, and it is clear precisely because the treatment was to *remove a capability* rather than govern its use. A capability that no longer exists does not need an operating history to be trusted.

**R-01 — payment-page script injection, High 20 — is held**, despite 6.4.3 being fully implemented, because the detective half of the control does not land until Phase 06.

## The lesson carried from Phase 03 — ADR-0012

Phase 03 learned that a configuration review can examine a correct documented standard while the estate has silently diverged from it. That lesson is now a rule:

> **A baseline document is not a control. The deployed state is the control, and measuring it is what makes the claim real.**

So compliance is evidenced from deployed state in three recorded classes — **asserted** (56 components, machine-evaluated continuously), **observed** (9 that cannot accept an agent), **attested** (6 SaaS control planes). Reporting a single coverage percentage would have hidden the nine. Quarterly **negative testing** confirms the assertions would actually detect a divergence: first cycle, 18 of 18 detected, two detection windows corrected.

## The honest position

Requirements 2 through 6 are implemented across all 71 assessed components. Three unauthorised scripts were found by the entity's own control rather than by a tester, and the path that produced them is closed rather than supervised.

What is **not** yet established is that any of it works under pressure. The controls are three months old, the patch SLA has never survived a seasonal freeze, one secure-development training cycle is not a culture, and the payment-page control is missing its detective half until Phase 06. The September penetration test is the first real examination of most of this.

Two items are already known to be heading for the assessment as findings: the **two legacy batch integrations with embedded credentials** (8.6.2) and the **nine appliances that cannot accept credentialed assessment** (CAP-06). Both are recorded now rather than discovered later.
